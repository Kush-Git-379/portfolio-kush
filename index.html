About Me

Engineer by training,
builder by instinct.

I'm Kush Mehta, an Electronics & Communication Engineering student at Nirma University (B.Tech, Aug 2023 – May 2027) with a minor in Data Science. My work sits at the intersection of hardware and software — designing circuits that fly, programming microcontrollers that sense, and writing code that makes sense of data.

As Electronics Lead for Team Arrow, Nirma's official aeromodelling and UAV club, I've led electronics design and integration for competition aircraft across three years and five SAE and Technoxian events — earning AIR 2 and AIR 3 nationally and a 5th-place international mission score at SAE Aero Design West 2025 in California.

In mid-2026 I spent ten weeks at the Space Applications Centre, ISRO, in the Navigation Receiver Division — building a real-time Galileo High Accuracy Service decoding utility in C++, implementing Reed–Solomon erasure decoding over GF(256) from the ICD, and validating the output end-to-end in RTKLIB to achieve centimetre-level Precise Point Positioning.

Alongside the flight work, I benchmark lightweight cryptography for constrained devices, run microarchitectural simulations in gem5, and build classical computer-vision systems. I'm applying for an MS in Robotics, Autonomous Systems, and Embedded Systems for entry after graduation.

In Progress
Simulation & Comparative Analysis of Lightweight Cryptography Techniques for Embedded Systems

A systematic study comparing AES-128-GCM, DES-CBC, ECC P-256, and ASCON-128 on throughput, peak memory, and energy efficiency across 5,000 IoT payloads at 32B, 64B, and 128B sizes. ASCON is loaded as a compiled C DLL via ctypes for native-speed benchmarking, with memory profiled using Python tracemalloc and significance tested over 10 trial runs. Findings support ASCON-128 as the NIST-recommended lightweight standard for resource-constrained edge devices. Conducted as part of Research Methodology & Seminar coursework at Nirma University.

Jamming Utilization: An Evaluation Hazard in Learned Frequency-Hopping Receivers

Co-authored with Mishri Bhavsar, Preya Parikh, and Prof. Sachin Gajjar (Nirma University). The paper shows that a conventionally trained learned hop detector reports higher accuracy under a follower jammer (0.842) than with no jammer at all (0.712) — not because it is robust, but because it is reading the jammer's own emission as the signal. Restricted to packets where the jammer targeted a decoy, the same detector finds the true hop on 1.8% of packets, statistically indistinguishable from the 1.6% chance level of a 64-slot band. Because the follower jammer produces this distortion while emitting 8.65× less energy than a barrage jammer, raw accuracy also reverses the apparent ranking of the two attacks. We recommend reporting a decoy-conditioned control alongside raw accuracy for any learned frequency-hopping receiver evaluated against a reactive adversary.
